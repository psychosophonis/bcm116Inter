Multipanel and panoramic video for VR

Here’s what you’ll make. You'll be able to place 'screens' or 'panels' of video around a VR space so the viewer can look from video to video. You can make these continuous and play with that, or have panels with characters who interact across the space. There are lots of possibilities and lots of potential to use the other AE tutorials to collage images and video (and sound) in 360 space. We will also use this technique to explore creative panoramas.

Why we’re doing this. A 360° video has no frame edge, so the viewer decides where to look. That means the first decision in any 360° work is where you put things around the viewer, which is what prompts 4 and 7 ask you to think about. Starting each copy at a different point in its loop is also the simplest way to get difference out of repetition, which is prompt 9.

What you’ll come away with. You’ll understand how a flat frame twice as wide as it is tall wraps around the viewer: where straight ahead is, where behind is, and why the left and right edges join up. You’ll know how to start copies at different points without breaking the loop, and which Premiere setting makes YouTube treat your file as 360°. You’ll also have a template you can drop your own loops into.

You'll learn about panorama and 360 video. You could start with a panoramic photograph. You can make a very high resolution one by following the instructions over here. That said, I'd prefer you to make something less veridical and more creative.

How a flat 2:1 frame becomes 360°

All a 360 photograph or panorama is, is an image with the ratio 2:1 that is wrapped in a sphere around the VR space. This is wonderful - it means we can make panoramas by hacking or collaging them together from any media at all. This is so much more interesting to me than taking an Insta360 video ... but we have them too ... unravelled they are just 2:1, the same as all the others.

You build the piece on a flat canvas that’s twice as wide as it is tall. Imagine wrapping that frame around the viewer so its left and right edges meet. Moving across the frame is turning around: the full 6000 pixels is 360°, so a 750-pixel panel fills 45° of your view. Moving up the frame is looking up: the 3000 pixels run from straight down at the bottom to straight up at the top. The horizon line runs through the middle, at eye level. The squares are numbered the same way in both drawings, so you can match them up.

This is the actual file, laid out flat. No square crosses the edges, so there’s no seam to hide.

Part 1Make the 360° canvas in Premiere

Everything on this card happens in Premiere: you make the canvas, arrange your video on it by eye, and export it as 360°. Bring in any video you like: your After Effects loops, footage you shot, or both.

V1 · Make the canvas and bring in your video 2 steps

  1. V-01

    Open Premiere Pro and make a new project, ticking Skip import mode in the New Project dialog. Then choose File → New → Sequence. On the Settings tab, set Editing Mode to Custom, Frame Size to 6000 × 3000 and Pixel Aspect Ratio to Square Pixels. Then click the VR Video tab and set Projection to Equirectangular and Layout to Monoscopic.

    Why is it twice as wide as it is tall? All panorama is 2:1. Across the canvas is turning all the way round; up and down is looking from your feet to the sky. The empty canvas is black, which is the space your video hangs in.
  2. V-02

    Import your video: in the Project panel, double-click where it says to import media, or choose File → Import. Drag the first clip onto track V1. It lands in the middle of the canvas, which is where the viewer is facing when the video opens.

Is your video not ready yet? You can start with one of these: nested shapes, vertical lines or radial lines. Download one and import it at V-02.

Part 2Arrange your video around the viewer

Arrange by eye: drag the clips into place, size them, then look at it in 360° and adjust.

V2 · Place the panels 3 steps

  1. V-03

    Drag each of your other clips into the sequence on its own track, stacked up: the next on V2, then V3, and so on. To use the same clip again, hold ⌥ Option and drag it up a track to copy it. If you run out of tracks, drop the clip in the empty space above the top one and Premiere adds a track.

  2. V-04

    Turn on snapping: click the spanner icon at the bottom right of the Program Monitor and choose Snap in Program Monitor.

    Then click a clip in the Program Monitor. Drag it into place, and drag one of its corner handles to make it the size you want. Do the same with each clip. Keep the panels on the middle line and spread them across the canvas.

    How big? The canvas is the whole way round, so a panel an eighth of the canvas wide fills about as much of your view as a TV across the room.
    Keep them off the left and right edges. The edges join up behind the viewer, and a panel across them gets cut in two. Leave a bit of black at each edge and you never have to deal with the join.
  3. V-05

    Click the spanner icon at the bottom right of the Program Monitor and choose VR Video → Enable. Drag with the mouse to look around. Turn it off, move things, and look again until the space feels right.

Want it to loop without a jump? Do the looping in After Effects: loop your clip there (Interpret Footage → Loop) and render it long, as in the After Effects workshop. In Premiere, make every panel the same length as your sequence.

Part 3Export as 360°

Export and upload exactly as in the panorama card: P-06 (export with VR Video ticked) and P-07 (upload and test in a headset).
