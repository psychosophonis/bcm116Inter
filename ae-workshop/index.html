After Effects as a painterly process

An introduction to procedural generation and After Effects. This step by step should give you an introduction to using After Effects as a creative, painterly tool. In this tutorial I develop a short looping three-screen work, using difference and repetition to create novel forms. In the next few weeks we will look at how to assemble multiscreen, 360 and projection works. Everything here is made in a square frame, which works on the dome and can be placed on a screen later.

Be sceptical of fireworks. Some combinations produce impressive emergent effects with no effort — instant symmetry, endless spirals. They are spectacular and limited as a means of individual expression. For anything you make, ask: could someone else have got this from the same settings? If yes, it is fireworks. What keeps a piece yours is almost always the source: the mark you painted, the thing you found, the footage you shot.

Path AWatercolour and repetition

A watercolour mark, repeated around a centre. Where copies overlap, the colour builds.

A1 · Make and capture 2 steps

  1. A-01

    Paint three patterns on white paper: one of nested shapes of colour, one of vertical lines, one of radial lines extending from a point.

    Light washes work well. Multiply keeps them as painted, and where copies overlap they deepen.
  2. A-02

    Photograph them with your phone held parallel to the paper, in even light, with no shadow falling across the sheet. One photo of the whole sheet is fine.

A2 · Set up 3 steps

  1. A-03

    Get the photo onto your computer. Open After Effects — the app itself, not After Effects Render Engine, which installs alongside it. Choose New Project.

  2. A-04

    Click the New Composition button in the middle of the screen. Set Width 2048, Height 2048, 30 fps, duration 10 s.

    A square fits the dome as it is, and can be placed inside a wide screen frame later.

  3. A-05

    Add a background: Layer → New → Solid, comp size, white.

A3 · Bring in the mark 5 steps

  1. A-06

    Drag your photo from Finder straight into the timeline, above the solid.

  2. A-07

    Move and scale. With the Selection tool, drag the photo so the mark you want to work with sits in the top half of the frame. Then in the Properties panel (Window → Properties if it isn't showing) drag Scale down until that mark takes up a quarter of the frame at most.

    Properties panel to set a value; the Transform twirl in the timeline to animate one. You'll use both.
    This may take some back and forth. The mark drifts a little as you scale. It doesn't need to be precise — it will work anyway.
    Why the top half. Copies will turn around the centre of the frame, so a mark sitting at the centre just spins in place.
    Why small. The mark is about to be copied round a circle. Too big and the copies merge into one blob.
  3. A-08

    Make the paper white. On the photo layer: Effect → Color Correction → Levels. Drag the white input marker left, watching the comp, until the edge of the photo disappears into the white background.

    Keep going a little further, and choose a setting that gives your paint some luminous transparency. Stop before the colour washes out.

  4. A-09

    Isolate one mark. With the photo layer selected, pick the Rectangle tool and drag a box around one mark. The box becomes a mask: only what's inside it shows. It doesn't need to be tight.

    The layer must be selected. With nothing selected, the Rectangle tool makes a new shape layer instead of a mask.
  5. A-10

    Put the anchor point on the mark: Layer → Transform → Center Anchor Point in Layer Content. Check the small cross lands on your mark.

    Why. A layer rotates and scales around its anchor point, which starts at the centre of the whole photo. Leave it there and the mark swings in an arc instead of spinning.
  6. ✓

    Checkpoint. One mark on the white background, spinning point on the mark, no visible edge of the photo.

A4 · A simple rotation 1 step

  1. A-11

    To animate, twirl open the layer's Transform in the timeline. At frame 0, click the stopwatch next to Rotation. Go to the last frame and set Rotation to 360. Press the spacebar to play: the mark spins in place.

A5 · Pre-compose: a strategy 1 step

  1. A-12

    Select the mark's layer. Layer → Pre-compose, and choose Move all attributes into the new composition.

    Your spinning mark is now a single layer. Inside it, nothing has changed. Outside, you can treat it as material: copy it, turn it, scale it — without touching the animation. Change the animation once, inside, and every copy updates.

    This is called nesting: a composition used as a layer inside another. Use it whenever something works and you want more of it.

    Why it matters here. Each copy in the ring needs two rotations: its own spin, and a different turn around the centre. A layer can hold only one Rotation. Nesting puts the spin inside and leaves the pre-comp layer free to be turned.

A6 · Around a centre 3 steps

  1. A-13

    Select the pre-comp layer and Edit → Duplicate (or ⌘D) until you have 12 copies.

  2. A-14

    Select each copy in turn and set its Rotation in the Properties panel, 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210, 240, 270, 300, 330. Each is 30° further round — 360 ÷ 12.

    Copy cut off at the edge? The mark has moved outside the pre-comp's frame. Open the pre-comp and bring it back in, or make the pre-comp larger in Composition → Composition Settings.
  3. A-15

    Look where copies overlap: each one's white paper covers the marks beneath it. Select all the copies and set their mode to Multiply. If you can't see a Mode column, right-click any column heading in the timeline and tick Modes.

    The paper disappears and the overlaps build colour instead.

    What Multiply does. It combines each layer with what's beneath it, darkening only. White changes nothing, so the paper vanishes; pigment over pigment gets denser.
  4. ✓

    Checkpoint. Copies arranged round the centre. Where they overlap, the colour gets denser.

A7 · Kaleidoscope behind 4 steps

  1. A-16

    Select all 12 copies — not the white solid. Layer → Pre-compose, Move all attributes into the new composition. The whole ring is now one layer.

    Set the new layer's mode to Multiply.

    Leave the solid out. If it goes inside, the ring comes back as a white square.
  2. A-17

    Select the ring layer and Edit → Duplicate (or ⌘D). You now have two identical rings, both on Multiply.

  3. A-18

    Select the lower ring layer — the one behind. Effect → Stylize → CC Kaleida. Change Mirroring until you like the pattern.

  4. A-19

    In the Properties panel, adjust Opacity on both layers until each is readable: your ring in front, the kaleidoscope as a ground behind it.

    Kaleida works better behind than alone. On its own it makes anything look impressive and nothing look like yours. Behind your ring, it becomes texture for your mark.
  5. ✓

    Checkpoint. Your ring clearly in front, a softer kaleidoscope behind it, both moving.

Path BRepeater and track matte

A drawn shape, multiplied by the Repeater, used as a window onto video. The shape alone is fireworks. The footage is where the piece becomes yours — shoot your own.

B1 · A spinning square 5 steps

  1. B-01

    Composition → New Composition. Width 2048, Height 2048, 30 fps, duration 10 s.

  2. B-02

    Add a background: Layer → New → Solid, comp size, black.

  3. B-03

    Click the empty grey area below the layers in the timeline, so nothing is selected. Select the Rectangle tool (or another shape tool). Change Fill and Stroke to white in the toolbar. Draw a small rectangle just above the centre of the screen. A Shape Layer 1 appears.

    Nothing selected, or you'll get a mask. With the solid selected, the shape tool cuts a mask into it instead of making a new shape layer.
  4. B-04

    Put the anchor point on the square: Layer → Transform → Center Anchor Point in Layer Content. Check the small cross lands on the square.

    Why. The square turns around its anchor point. Leave it where it is and the square swings in an arc instead of spinning.
  5. B-05

    Spin the square. Twirl open Shape Layer 1 → Contents → Rectangle 1 → Transform: Rectangle 1. At frame 0, click the stopwatch next to Rotation. Go to the last frame and set it to 360.

    Every copy will carry this spin. The Repeater copies the square as it is, so every square in the ring turns on the spot.

B2 · Repeaters 4 steps

  1. B-06

    On the Contents row, click Add → Repeater. Set Copies to 30.

  2. B-07

    Open Repeater 1 → Transform: Repeater. Set Position to 0, 0 and Rotation to 12.

    Position must be 0, 0 for a ring. Any other value and the copies march off in a line.
  3. B-08

    Centre and scale the ring. With Shape Layer 1 selected: Layer → Transform → Center Anchor Point in Layer Content. Then in the Properties panel set Position to 1024, 1024, and drag Scale down until the ring sits small and tight around the centre.

  4. B-09

    On the Contents row, click Add → Repeater again. Open Repeater 2 → Transform: Repeater. Set Copies 16 and Position 0, 0.

    Play with Scale until the rings are spaced out across the frame. Then play with Rotation to offset each ring from the one inside it.

    A Repeater copies everything above it. Repeater 1 copies the square into a ring. Repeater 2 sits below it, so it copies the whole ring — each copy scaled and turned a little more than the last.

B3 · A loop 2 steps

  1. B-10

    Rotate the whole array. Twirl open Shape Layer 1 → Transform in the timeline. At frame 0, click the stopwatch next to Rotation. Go to the last frame and set it to 360. Press the spacebar to play: the whole array turns on its centre.

  2. B-11

    Make it flow. Open Repeater 2. At frame 0, click the stopwatch next to Offset (it reads 0). Go to the last frame and set Offset to 1. Play it.

    What Offset does. It slides every copy along to the next copy's place. From 0 to 1, each ring grows into the one outside it, so the last frame matches the first and the loop runs forever — whatever scale and spacing you chose.
    A ring pops in at the centre on the loop? The innermost ring is too big to hide the join. Scale the ring smaller at B-08.
  3. ✓

    Checkpoint. A dense array of rings from the centre to the edge, turning and flowing outward, looping without a jump.

B4 · Video through the pattern 6 steps

  1. B-12

    Import your video and drag it into the timeline directly below the shape layer.

  2. B-13

    Zoom the first video. With it selected, drag Scale up in the Properties panel until the video fills the square frame.

  3. B-14

    Select the video layer. Layer → Track Matte → Matte with Layer Above → Alpha Matte. The video now shows only through the pattern.

    Still seeing all the video? The shape layer's eye should have switched off by itself. If it's still on, the matte isn't applied.
  4. B-15

    If the video is shorter than the rotation, select the matted video layer and duplicate it (⌘D). Drag the duplicate along the timeline so it follows on from the first, until video covers the whole rotation.

  5. B-16

    Import a second, related video and put it just above the black solid. Then zoom it: with it selected, drag Scale up in the Properties panel until it fills the square frame. Now the pattern is a window from one video onto another.

  6. B-17

    If the second video is shorter than the rotation, select it and duplicate it (⌘D). Drag the duplicate along the timeline so it follows on from the first, until video covers the whole rotation.

  7. ✓

    Checkpoint. One video seen through the spinning ring, another around it.

FinishExport

The same for either path. The square is ready for the dome as it is. For a screen, place it inside a wide frame first.

E1 · Render the square 4 steps

  1. E-01

    With your main composition open: Composition → Add to Render Queue. The Render Queue panel opens.

    Render the main composition, not a pre-comp. If you're looking inside a pre-comp, click the main composition's tab at the top of the timeline first.
  2. E-02

    Click the blue text next to Output Module. Set Format to H.264. Click OK.

  3. E-03

    Click the blue text next to Output To. Name the file and choose where to save it.

  4. E-04

    Click Render. Wait for the bar to finish.

    It renders the work area. That's the bar across the top of the timeline. If you've shortened it, drag it back out to the full 10 seconds, or the loop will be cut short.
  5. ✓

    Checkpoint. Open the file and set it to loop. It plays round without a jump.

E2 · For a screen 3 steps

  1. E-05

    Composition → New Composition. Width 1920, Height 1080, 30 fps, duration 10 s. Add a solid the same colour as your background: Layer → New → Solid.

  2. E-06

    Drag your square composition from the Project panel into the timeline, above the solid. In the Properties panel, drag Scale down until the whole square fits top to bottom.

    Your square is now a layer. The same nesting as pre-composing: the whole piece, animation and all, placed in a new frame.
  3. E-07

    Render it as in E-01 to E-04, with this wide composition open.

  4. ✓

    Checkpoint. A wide file with your square piece centred in it, looping without a jump.

CheckSee it on the dome

Render one frame: Composition → Save Frame As → File, PNG. Drop it into a dome simulator and look up.

  • Dome viewer — stills. The camera stays at the dome's centre, so what you see is what the dome does.

Before you look: the centre of your square is straight up, and distance from the centre is how far down the dome. Marks near the edge stretch — that's the dome, not an error. Keep important things within about half the radius. For the planetarium itself, check what they want delivered — often a numbered image sequence rather than a video, which is set in the same Output Module under Format.
